Leverage lens: do the people who matter in-country want it?

Framing from Lant Pritchett, "The Death of Aid Was Political Suicide" (In Development): aid built around targeted harm-reduction "does not focus on those with power in recipient countries" and so buys little influence. Aid that matches a country's own agenda (growth, infrastructure, security) does. This chart asks two judgment questions per block: how much do in-country power brokers want it (x), and how much does it hand the US a gate or condition (y)? Leverage needs both.

Hooks (top right) are the leverage engine: wanted and conditional. Gifts (bottom right) are wanted but give the US nothing to hold. Discipline (top left) is what makes the offer scarce and credible, but elites tolerate it only if the prize is big enough. The blocks with the most gate (selectivity, ERR, clock, tranching, fiduciary controls) are the ones in-country power brokers like least, so leverage rests on how big and how wanted the prize is. Numbers on the chart match the row order in the matrix above.

Everything on this chart is judgment. The only sourced evidence on in-country response is the MCC-effect work: reform in 45 of 118 candidates (38%), running through domestic reformers and a "seal of approval". Scores will differ by country, and a government is not the same as the people who hold power.

How to read the evidence

Hidden load. The growth-diagnostics block scores high with economists and near zero with State, OMB and the 2025 CSIS and Atlantic Council papers (the CSIS piece does not mention constraints analysis). That is the block most likely to be pulled without anyone noticing, which is the Jenga point.

Loosening pressure. Several proposed additions weaken load-bearing blocks: board discretion in place of the scorecard, a national-security waiver, tiered scorecards, procurement preferences. Each one trades selectivity or the returns hurdle for leverage.
